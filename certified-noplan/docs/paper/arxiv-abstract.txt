When a motion planner fails to find a path, it produces no evidence that none exists. Yet task-and-motion planners pruning a search, and safety engineers arguing that a robot cannot reach an operator zone, need a proof. Existing infeasibility proofs for manipulators rest on floating-point pipelines that the consumer must trust as a whole. We present, to our knowledge, the first infeasibility certificates for motion planning of articulated arms that are exactly re-verifiable a posteriori: a 499-line standard-library verifier, computing in exact rational arithmetic, re-derives the robot's kinematics from the certificate and checks the proof without trusting the planner, the LP solver or any collision checker. The method combines a rational parametrization of forward kinematics, a low-degree barrier whose slab separates start from goal inside the obstacle region, and per-cell witnesses found by linear programming over Bernstein coefficients; no semidefinite program enters the trust chain, and making a certificate exact reduces to rounding rationals. On a laptop CPU we certify a KUKA iiwa7 whose kinematics reproduce the published URDF to $2\cdot10^{-6}$ m and whose trapped link carries its faithful convex silhouette, unable to reach a target behind an overhead shelf, in under a minute; the verifier re-checks it in about 2.4 s. Cost is governed by the number of active dimensions of the trapped body, not total DOF: redundant joints are covered by the theorem at negligible cost ($443\times$ smaller per-leaf LP on the 7-DOF flagship). We also report two cases where dense sampling vouched for a disconnection that did not exist, and the certifier refused.
